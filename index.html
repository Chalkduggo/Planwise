Common questions

Is my data safe?

Everything you type stays in your browser. A salary slip is read on your own device. If you choose to save a plan, it is stored in this browser only.

Is this financial advice?

No. Planwise gives educational estimates based on the numbers and assumptions you enter. Returns and prices are not guaranteed, so please speak to a qualified professional before you decide.

Where do the tax numbers come from?

From the table in Tax rules. The defaults follow published slabs for people under 60. Please confirm them and edit the table if the rules change.

Can I keep my plan and come back later?

Yes. Save it in this browser, download a file, or copy the data. You can also print it or save it as a PDF.

Where should I start?

Try the 5-year plan if you have a salary slip handy. Otherwise open the emergency fund and try different savings rates.

    Explain it simply

    No jargon. Every new word is explained like you are learning it for the first time. Pick a topic, or read them in order.

    Money basics: coming in, going out, left over

    Money comes in. That is your income. It is the pay you get for your work.

    Money goes out. That is your spending. It pays for food, a home, the bus and some fun.

    What is left over is money you can keep. Keeping it is called saving.

    Think of a water tank. One tap fills it and another tap empties it. If the filling tap is bigger, the tank grows. Saving is leaving some water in the tank.

    ExampleYou get ₹100. You spend ₹70. You keep ₹30. The ₹30 is your savings.

    Emergency fund: an umbrella for money

    An emergency fund is money you keep aside for bad surprises. A surprise could be losing your job, a hospital visit, or a broken scooter you need to get to work.

    It is like an umbrella. You do not use it every day. But when it rains, you are very glad it is there.

    How much? Many people keep enough for 6 to 8 months of the things they must pay for, like food, rent and bills.

    Why do it first? If you put all your money into things that go up and down, and a surprise comes, you may have to sell at a bad time. The umbrella protects you.

    ExampleYour must-have costs are ₹10 a month. Six months of cover means ₹60 in the umbrella.

    Where to keep your emergency fund

    Some of the money should be easy to grab right now. Some can sit somewhere that pays a little more but takes a day to reach.

    Think of coins in your pocket, a drawer and a cupboard. The pocket is quickest. The cupboard takes longer but may be safer from spending.

    A savings account is the pocket. A sweep-in FD or a liquid fund is the drawer. A short FD is the cupboard. Planwise lets you choose how to split the money.

    Interest, growth and inflation

    Interest is a thank-you for lending your money. When you keep money in a bank, the bank uses it and pays you a little extra.

    Compounding means the extra also earns extra. It is like a snowball. As it rolls it picks up more snow, so it grows faster and faster.

    Inflation means prices slowly go up. A sweet that costs ₹1 today may cost ₹2 one day. If your money grows slower than prices, it buys less every year.

    ExamplePrices rise 6% a year. Your savings earn 4%. Each year your money buys a little less, even though the number looks bigger.

    Bond ladder: money that pays you every month

    A fixed deposit (FD) is lending money to a bank for a set time. The bank promises to pay extra and give your money back at the end.

    A bond is similar. You lend to a company or a government. They pay you interest. On the day it ends, called maturity, they give your money back.

    A ladder splits your money into small pieces called tranches. You start one piece each month, like the rungs of a ladder.

    After a year, one piece finishes every month. You get money back with interest. You can spend the interest or start a new piece at the top.

    It is like a bus stop. Instead of one big bus a year, a small bus comes every month.

    The yield is the yearly extra you earn. A 7% yield means ₹7 a year for every ₹100.

    Funds and small-cap: growing, but bumpy

    A mutual fund is a big basket. Many people put money in, and a manager buys lots of things with it.

    A small-cap fund buys small companies. Small companies can grow fast, but they wobble a lot. They are like a young plant. It may grow tall, or the wind may knock it down.

    Risk means you can get back less than you put in. Never put money you need soon into something that wobbles.

    In Planwise, only the extra yield above your income need goes into the small-cap fund.

    Chance of success: rolling the dice many times

    Nobody knows the future. So Planwise plays the future thousands of times, each time with different random ups and downs. It is like rolling dice 2,000 times.

    Then it counts. In how many games did you end with at least what you put in? That count is your chance of success.

    The bad case means 1 game in 10 did worse than that. The good case means 1 game in 10 did better.

    The yearly swing, also called volatility, says how wildly prices bounce. A bigger swing means more surprises both ways.

    This shows a range of possible outcomes. It is not a promise.

    Reading a salary slip

    A salary slip is a note that shows how your pay is shared out each month.

    • Gross pay is your pay before anything is taken away.
    • Net pay or take-home is what lands in your bank.
    • PF (provident fund) is money taken from your pay and kept for when you are old.
    • TDS is tax taken from your pay before you get it.
    • CTC is the total yearly cost of you to your company. It includes things you never see in your bank.
    ExampleGross ₹100. PF takes ₹10. Tax takes ₹10. Your take-home is ₹80.

    Income tax: sharing with the country

    Tax is a share of what you earn that goes to the government. It pays for roads, schools and hospitals.

    Tax works like stairs. The first steps are free or cheap. Higher steps cost a bigger share. Each step is called a bracket or slab. Only the money on a step is charged that step’s rate, not all of your money.

    A deduction is a discount. Some of your income is simply not counted. A rebate is an extra discount that can bring the tax to zero for smaller incomes.

    Your effective rate is the share of all your income that went in tax. Your marginal rate is the share on the next bit you earn. They are not the same.

    In India you can pick the old regime (more deductions, higher rates) or the new regime (fewer deductions, lower rates). Planwise compares them for you. The United States and each European country have their own steps, so you can choose yours.

    ExampleSay the first ₹40 is free and the next ₹40 is taxed at 10%. If you earn ₹60, only ₹20 is taxed, so the tax is ₹2. It is not 10% of ₹60.

    The 5-year plan

    Planwise looks at your pay and your costs, then guesses the next five years.

    Each year your pay may go up. That is your raise. Your costs also go up. That is your spending growth. The money left over each month is your free money.

    The plan does one thing first: it fills the umbrella (your emergency fund). Out of every ₹100 you invest, about ₹90 goes there until it is 90% full. Then the ladder starts and gets the rest. When the umbrella is full, all of it goes to the ladder.

    The ladder pays interest every month. One day that interest can pay part of your bills. That is the steady income you are building.

    The stress test asks: if my pay stopped today, how many months could I pay my must-have costs?

    Purchases planner: saving for the next phone

    Things wear out. Your phone may last three years. The next one may cost more.

    Instead of paying all at once, you drop a little money into a jar each month. When the day comes, the jar is full and you do not need to worry.

    Planwise counts what you will get for selling the old one, and that prices may rise. It tells you how much to put in the jar each month.

    ExampleA ₹36 phone in 36 months needs about ₹1 a month. Prices rising adds a little more.

    Calendar and the calendar file

    The calendar shows your month. Blue is money coming in. Orange is bills you must pay. Green is money you move to savings.

    Tap a day to open it and see exactly where your money goes that day.

    The calendar file (.ics) is a small file you can open in your phone’s calendar. After that, your phone reminds you the day before each bill or transfer. It is like an alarm clock for money jobs.

    What-if stories: scenarios

    A scenario is a “what if” story. What if my raise is smaller? What if the yield is higher?

    It is like trying on two jackets before you choose one. Planwise runs your whole plan again for each story and shows the results side by side. The best number in each row is shaded.

    Insurance: sharing big risks

    Many people each pay a small fee to a company. This fee is called a premium. If one of them has a big problem, the company pays. The risk is shared.

    Health insurance helps pay hospital bills. Term life insurance pays your family if you die while you are covered.

    Some things are not covered for a while after you start. That is the waiting period. Cover from your job may end if you leave, so a personal policy can be useful.

    Planwise does a rough check and tells you if your cover looks far below what you chose.

    The currency box

    Different countries use different money. India uses rupees, the United States uses dollars and many European countries use euros.

    The currency box only changes the sign and the way numbers are written. It does not turn 100 rupees into dollars. Type your amounts in the money you use.

    Your data, saving and sharing

    What you type stays in your browser. It is like writing in a notebook that you keep in your own bag.

    • Save in this browser puts the notebook in a drawer on this device only. Clearing your browser data can erase it.
    • Download file lets you keep a copy somewhere else.
    • Share link packs your numbers into a web address. Anyone who has the link can read them, so only send it to people you trust.
